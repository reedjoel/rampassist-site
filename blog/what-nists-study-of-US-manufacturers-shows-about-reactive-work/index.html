Most Maintenance Teams Want More Planned Work.
Here's Where the Hours Are Going.

What NIST's study of U.S. manufacturers shows about reactive work, and where diagnosis time fits in

Most maintenance programs share the goal of shifting more work from reactive to planned. Research from the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) shows why the shift can be difficult. In its 2020 study of U.S. manufacturers, NIST found that on average 45.7% of machinery maintenance was reactive, and the plants that relied most on reactive maintenance experienced 3.3 times more downtime and 16 times more defects than the plants that relied on it least.

About the NIST study

NIST surveyed U.S. discrete manufacturers on their maintenance practices. On average, their maintenance split 45.7% reactive, 31.8% preventive, and 17.3% predictive. The downtime and defect comparisons set the top quarter of reactive-maintenance users against the bottom quarter. Source: Douglas S. Thomas and Brian A. Weiss, Economics of Manufacturing Machinery Maintenance, NIST AMS 100-34 (2020).

The gap has little to do with knowledge or effort. Reactive work draws on the same people and the same hours that planned work depends on. When a line goes down, the technician scheduled for a PM gets pulled to the fault and the PM moves to another day. Stronger PM programs and reliability projects are the long-term answer, but they take time to pay back, and in the meantime close to half of the work is still reactive. Recovering time inside that reactive work is one of the most practical ways to protect the planned side while it grows.

Where Reactive Hours Actually Go

RAMP AI, a machine interpreter built for industrial maintenance teams, was designed around a specific view of how reactive time gets spent. Brent Bregar, RAMP AI's Chief Product Officer, spent more than twenty years troubleshooting machines on plant floors before helping build it.

"Most people think the only way to reduce MTTR is to reduce the repair time, but it's not the only way. You can also target the time between knowing there's a problem and knowing what it is. That's what we are solving."

— Brent Bregar, Chief Product Officer

He points to a saw system with a recurring sensor fault, where the hours went into tracing logic and working through alarms that pointed at symptoms.

One recurring fault

Each occurrence cost one to four hours on a line where downtime ran $40,000 to $60,000 an hour in lost profit. Once the team knew it was the sensor, replacing it took minutes.

Brent also describes the decision underneath many faults:

"If I have a $500 part that is suspected to be causing my problem, do I go grab another $500 part and put it in, or do I continue troubleshooting it? If the line costs $100,000 an hour of downtime, the obvious answer is I replace the part. But if the line is a simple line, I may troubleshoot it longer."

— Brent Bregar, Chief Product Officer

On an expensive line, swapping the part is the right call. The tradeoff is that the underlying condition doesn't always get confirmed, and if it returns, it becomes another reactive event. Teams make this decision every day with the information they have, and better information, sooner, changes the outcome.
