Every plant has one. A machine that runs fine for hours, then does something that makes no sense. You walk over, watch it, and it behaves perfectly. You walk away, and it happens again.

That's not bad luck. That's a ghost.

A Ghost Plays Tricks with Case Counts

A customer brought us a case packer that had been acting up for longer than anyone wanted to admit. It was supposed to pack twelve units a case. Most of the time it did. But every so often a case came off with eleven, and every so often one came off with thirteen.

There was no alarm and no fault code, and nobody could see a pattern.

The team had done what every good maintenance department does. They checked the sensors, adjusted the timing, and talked to the operators. They waited for it to happen while somebody was standing there. It never did.

Busting the Ghost

They sent us the machine's control code and asked one question: "Can your system find what we can't?"

RAMP AI treats the control code as the source of truth. Prints and manuals tell you how the machine was meant to work. The code tells you how it actually works. So instead of guessing, RAMP AI walked through the logic the way an experienced automation engineer would, looking at what could cause a miscount in both directions.

The Reveal

The ghost only came out in one mode, when a particular selector switch was in a particular position.

In that mode, the "case full" signal was racing against the next product dropping in. Sometimes the signal won and sometimes the product won.

Two ways to miscount

Thirteen: the next product slipped in first, so the case got one extra before the machine called it full.

Eleven: the signal fired twice, so the machine counted a case as full before it really was.

It was intermittent, depended on the mode, and was invisible unless you knew exactly where to look. That's why months of good troubleshooting never caught it. Nobody was doing anything wrong. The ghost was simply hiding where people don't normally look.

Why Ghosts Are So Expensive

The common faults are the ones your techs already know cold. The rare one is the one that eats a whole shift, because nobody has seen it before.

"A one-in-a-hundred fault is a hundred percent of your problem on the day it happens."

— Brent Bregar, RAMP AI

RAMP AI gives the rare fault the same weight as the common one. It hands your tech a ranked list of possibilities, with the reasoning behind each one, so they never walk away empty-handed. Then it trusts their training to take it from there. Think of it as an experienced automation engineer looking over the shoulder of every maintenance person on your floor.
